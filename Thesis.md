Context is a $500B dollar cost doubling every 18 months. For every $1 spent on tokens that read context, $16 of human time went into producing it. Full, relevant context for these superintelligent systems is lacking today, but people are able to work around it in varying ways. However, once these systems accrue assets, and more importantly, initiate liabilities, on our behalf, full context is a fundamental necessity, yet insufficient by itself. What's additionally required is a model that can genuinely simulate how one would respond to a given situation.

Temporally interleaved, language-based, read-write action streams from daily computer use is a good place to start. Next action prediction is the best forcing function for maximizing user trust and proving the ability to actually convert that context into something tangible and useful. A model trained in this way would learn how one thinks at a fundamental level; it would be able to simulate how one converts inbound information into outbound action, and it would need to maintain an implicit understanding of user goals in order to minimize loss. 

The first step to a model like this involves monitoring and processing inbound information and outbound action, and using continually retrained behavior cloning on outputs conditioned on inputs. This requires zero manual labeling or workflow changes for the user. Second, sample the human model and display its results as suggestions. The user's continued work would act as a positive sample in contrast to the hard negative samples in pairwise preference optimizations via DPO variants to surpass demonstrator performance. 

The result is a human model that can accelerate local workflows as long as it remains in touch with ones context, and is aware of what those goals are as a result. This model can then be utilized to participate in the collective intelligence of an organization, the agentic economy as a whole, etc, with true alignment, since its literally trained to respond as one would to a given situation.

There will always be a large market of people who want to own the human model that is privy to the full context of their work, while portioning off slices of that valuable resource to closed providers when needed to accomplish a local objective. These people should spend zero time needing to explain context or goals to utilize intelligence. The intelligence should be able to digest full context and understand implicit goals from that context, stepping in as needed to accelerate work.

The medium term future consists of billions of these models coordinating in a collective intelligence that dwarfs the output of today's world in the same way that today's world dwarfs the output from 1900. Unless your full context, values, and goals are actually baked into weights via continuous interaction, you won't exist in that world.



# Alternative Thesis

**Intelligence is getting cheaper. Context is getting more expensive.**

Businesses already pay an enormous context tax. It is paid in meetings, handoffs, search, documentation, prompting, repeated explanations, and work that must be redone because the person or system performing it did not know enough. AI does not automatically eliminate this tax. As models become more capable, the opportunity cost of missing context rises: more intelligence sits idle, produces generic work, or moves quickly in the wrong direction while people spend increasing amounts of time bringing it up to speed.

That problem becomes existential when agents stop merely answering questions and begin accumulating assets, making commitments, and initiating liabilities on our behalf. At that point, relevant context is not a convenience. It is a prerequisite for delegation. But context alone is insufficient. A useful agent must also understand how a particular person or organization turns information into judgment: what matters now, which constraints are implicit, what outcome counts as progress, and when a locally plausible action would violate a larger goal.

Today, people reconstruct fragments of that state every time they write a prompt. Memory systems preserve selected facts. Retrieval systems find prior text. Neither proves that a model understands the moving edge of the work. The forcing function is prediction: given what a person has read, written, seen, and done, can a model anticipate what they will do next?

Ordinary computer use already produces the beginning of this dataset. Documents are read, messages arrive, model outputs are considered, searches are made, notes are edited, and decisions become actions. When these events are captured as a temporally faithful stream, each bounded human action becomes supervision for how prior context became behavior. The first hypothesis is simple and falsifiable: correct personal context should predict future actions better than no context, stale context, or someone else's context. This can be tested before claiming that the system understands a person, knows their goals, or deserves authority.

If the predictive substrate works, the model can begin to participate. It samples possible next actions and presents a small number as suggestions. The person may use one, improve it, combine several, reject all of them, or take a different path. Their continued work creates local comparative signal without turning the day into a labeling exercise. A behavioral model learns how the person acts; a separate proposal policy learns to offer more useful possibilities. The objective is not to reproduce the demonstrator forever. It is to build a human-model system that reaches acceptable outcomes faster or better than the human alone or the human with a static assistant.

This creates a staged path from prediction to recommendation to agency. First, prove that the event stream contains useful personal signal. Second, prove that suggestions generated from that signal improve real work without narrowing judgment or manipulating the user. Only then extend the horizon: simulate short plans, expose predicted consequences and uncertainty, validate reversible actions in sandboxes, and execute only with explicit authority and rollback. Each phase has blockers that can invalidate or redirect the next. The vision is large because the upside is large, not because the intermediate problems are solved.

The initial product is a user-owned context layer for high-leverage teams. It continuously structures the work scattered across notes, browser activity, AI conversations, messages, meetings, and artifacts so that intelligence can enter with less repeated briefing and less loss between people, tools, and model providers. Next-action prediction measures whether that context is actually useful. Proactive assistance turns it into immediate leverage. Continual adaptation makes the system more valuable as the shared history grows.

Ownership matters. Frontier providers will continue to supply extraordinary general intelligence, but their economics encourage them to see the portion of context required for the current request. The complete history of how a person or company thinks is a different asset. Users should own that asset, control how it is learned from, and selectively expose the minimum context needed to outside models. Intelligence can be rented. Context, judgment, and organizational memory should compound locally.

The long-term result is not one monolithic company agent. It is a model for every participant, each grounded in the context and goals of the person it represents, coordinating with the models of teammates, customers, and counterparties. Organizations already create intelligence through many differently informed people working together. Billions of context-rich models can extend that structure into a collective intelligence vastly more capable than any isolated system, while keeping human authority and heterogeneous objectives inside the network.

Intelligence without context is generic. Context without a model is inert. The people and companies that turn their work into a continuously learning, user-owned representation of how they think will compound intelligence. Everyone else will repeatedly rent agents that must be taught who they are, what they know, and what they are trying to accomplish.
