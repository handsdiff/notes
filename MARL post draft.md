
It’s pretty clear that modern AI can, or will soon, generally achieve close to perfect function approximation and optimal decision making of any verifiable, stationary task we give it. I’d even consider non stationary environments where the extent of the non stationarity is itself stationary to be solved. If there’s fixed randomness, the agent will learn that and still perform optimally. What comes next is performing optimally in non stationary environments, where the non stationarity itself is non stationary. Put more simply, environments where other agents are also attempting to maximize their reward, changing their policy based on your actions.

You might point to AlphaGo’s 2016 victory or the man vs machine competitions of Starcraft and Dota and say that this distinction is irrelevant and we’re past this point already. Yet I wonder why my LLM can’t negotiate with my investors on my behalf during a fundraise or make money trading or investing or dominate the game of Mafia or engage successfully in persuasion to humans or find and convert leads for me or engage in sales or write good blog posts or tweets. It feels like there is a distinction between [2 player zero sum games](https://x.com/polynoamial/status/1980697004658556972) with a fixed action space and multiplayer general sum games with the action space of all language and code, which is where humans mostly play and the most valuable work occurs.

There are several examples that illuminate the gap. The former head of the $40B commodities desk at Bridgewater cannot trust LLMs to place trades since it lacks the ability to reason about the impact of its trades on the broader market. If someone puts in a 5 day TWAP to buy an asset, smart countertraders will buy a ton before you then dump on your head once you’ve finished buying. Sergey Levine’s paper I mentioned in a [previous article](https://handsdiff.substack.com/p/reasoning-through-agentic-memory) shows that introducing a world model of social dynamics to an LLM achieves SOTA performance on Avalon and persuasion tasks. Essentially, because frontier models lack the ability to reason through and predict the actions of other agents in its environment, it performs suboptimally.

One of the main reasons LLMs are so interesting in the domain of reinforcement learning is that for a long time, RL had extremely poor transfer. Optimizing an agent to maximize rewards in one environment did not help it perform better in another. In the past 7 years or so, we’ve collected a ton of empirical evidence for two foundational paradigms that help generalize RL, language and locomotion. While frontier lab employees have mentioned instances of poor transfer between RL tasks for LLMs, in general the reason labs focus so much on coding and math is because the logic learned there, mapped to language sequences, [applies to many other settings](https://x.com/trq212/status/2061907337154367865). Similarly for robotics, training a base model on all the possible tasks you could do, for example, in a kitchen [improves performance](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qNdsI_4AQJw&t=1889s) when compared to training a specific model to clean the dishes, or unload the dishwasher, or warm up food. This might seem simple in hindsight but it’s actually a huge breakthrough and speeds up learning across domains previously thought to be separate.

Given this, what is the gap between dominating popular video games and superhuman performance in multiplayer environments with huge action spaces and dynamic actors? This phenomenon is approached from a ton of different directions. The way that makes the most intuitive sense to me is that the LLM lacks a good and relevant world model, which predicts how the environment changes based on its own actions. This is why [frameworks](https://x.com/PrimeIntellect/status/2064818411948093531) like [ECHO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.24517) improve terminal use performance. When applied to other agents in the world, this is often phrased as theory of mind. The issue is that determining optimal actions in scenarios like these is recursive. If agent A is trying to predict agent B, and agent B is trying to predict agent A, then the agent predictions never reach a conclusion. Take the example of rock paper scissors. If agent A is reasoning through playing rock, but thinks agent B predicts that it will play rock and thus play paper, then it might conclude to play scissors to that it can win. But then its prediction of agent B would inform it that agent B would play rock if it played scissors, so it would conclude to play paper so as to beat rock. And so on forever.

Practically, it comes down to data availability and reward modeling. As a quick aside, frontier lab employees have mentioned to me they believe a greater risk to society than broad scale LLM misalignment with humanity is local incentives at for-profit corporations. Because test time scaling on coding and math logic into RSI is a good narrative for investors, shows up in benchmarks, and is relatively easy to collect data for, that’s what is worked on. This leaves potentially more useful, valuable research like realtime environments, modeling user behavior during a working session, or maximizing collaborative reward by the wayside. For instance, a less known fact is that interrupting a model while its thinking takes it out of distribution and causes it to perform poorly, since its training data doesn’t involve real time user interruption. Overall, if you look at the system cards of any of the frontier models, not a single one has 3+ player general sum games.

The issue is this stuff is prohibitively difficult to collect data for and reward properly, since its fundamentally dynamic. In games like AlphaGo, there is more or less a truthful answer, it’s just really hard to compute. Deep RL showed amazing results in being able to find truth better than humans by using value functions and optimistic exploration during test time, and that was a huge breakthrough. A model trained statically on Go, even with a dynamic opponent via self play, can perform extremely well in the real world since some moves are simply better than others, in any scenario. In a game like rock paper scissors, there is no truthful action if you assume competent counterparties. **There only exists truth at a higher level of abstraction, the strategy from which actions are chosen**. The mathematical solution to maximize terminal rewards in rock paper scissors with equally capable players is to play rock, paper, and scissors randomly with equal distribution. The way to break the earlier recursion I explained is to recognize what strategy your counterparty is employing at any given moment, and choose a strategy, rather than action, that maximizes reward as a result. If agent A recognizes that the strategy employed by agent B is to play to win based on its prediction of A, then the Nash equilibrium is an equal distribution over actions for each player.

For most useful applications where this type of intelligence is required, such as marketing, sales, negotiation, investing, fundraising, etc, the world is constantly changing and so the optimal strategy to employ is constantly changing. Batch trained models, which are all frontier models today, struggle because static training environments teach actions that optimize for its training set rather than teaching the model a set of strategies and the associated pattern matching to choose one over the other based on what its seeing from its environment.

What does this actually mean in practice? In simple terms, [classical RL](https://handsdiff.substack.com/p/deep-reinforcement-learning) associates rewards with states, and the agent is trained to maximize rewards over the time horizon of the task. In coding, this might mean teaching the agent the action of writing code to end up in a state where it compiles (reward). However, if the state transitions of the system are not solely conditioned on your actions, how can you learn reward maximization? It depends on the state transitions. If it’s static stochasticity, then over time you learn the stochasticity and take the average. If it’s dynamic stochasticity, then the state is conditioned on the actions of other agents in the system, and you need to learn not only your policy, but the policy of the other agents. That’s fine too. But what if the policies of other agents is a function of their expectation of your policy? You end up in a recursive computation like the rock paper scissors example.

Most _social intelligence_ scenarios, which I’ll use to distinguish from the _logical intelligence_ scenarios where LLMs are currently excellent at, are like this. Intuitively, if I’m engaging in social behavior, what I say/do will impact what people around me say/do. Unless I learn these dynamics, the possible state space accessible to me as an individual agent is closed off, leading to stunted maximal rewards. Additionally, it’s impossible to distill this intelligence into a model through long horizon coding, math, and administrative white collar work tasks since none of these tasks involve the process of engaging with, learning from, and convincing others to take certain actions to achieve longer term goals. Recursive self improvement will continue to make exponential progress on the goals we’ve set up for it, but will never be able to choose the targets for us, just like superintelligence cannot decide what kind of ice cream I like, even if it’s able to predict it. As a result, to advance progress on these axes, we need to put LLMs in scenarios where state transitions are conditioned on the joint action spaces of multiple learning agents, where social dynamics, reputation, and game theory come into play to achieve higher than individual rewards.

We can take the premise that jointly training agents to maximize collaborative rewards is useful, valuable, and worth working towards. It’s worth questioning what constraints lead humans to collaboration and whether those constraints apply to LLMs as well. This will inform what type of behavior we might see during future training, or the extent to which LLMs will collaborate with humans.
